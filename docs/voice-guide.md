# Grey Wellness Voice Guide

This guide defines Mónica Grey's editorial voice for Grey Wellness. Read it before drafting, translating, or substantially editing website copy, blog posts, newsletters, or social content.

The published posts in `en/blog/posts/` and `es/blog/posts/` are the primary examples. This guide describes their shared patterns; it does not replace Mónica's judgment or clinical review.

## Voice in one sentence

Write like a bilingual therapist sitting across from one person: understand what they are living through, explain why it happens in plain language, and show that meaningful change is possible.

## Core qualities

- **Warm and direct.** Address the reader as “you” or “tú.” Sound present and human, not institutional.
- **Clinically grounded, never clinical-sounding.** Explain evidence accurately without turning the article into a textbook.
- **Validating without minimizing.** Acknowledge that the experience is exhausting, frightening, or frustrating before offering advice.
- **Hopeful without overpromising.** Emphasize that patterns can change and effective help exists. Never promise a cure or guaranteed outcome.
- **Conversational and culturally natural.** Use everyday language, light humor, and recognizable situations rather than generic wellness language.
- **Respectful and inclusive.** Preserve the reader's dignity and avoid framing symptoms as personal failure.

## Typical article movement

### Current blog direction

- Prefer a concise, more academic and evidence-based tone for blog articles while keeping explanations accessible. Blog posts also serve as source material for weekly social content.
- Avoid repeating the same mechanism, reassurance, or recommendation across sections. For focused educational articles, aim for roughly 600–700 words unless the topic requires more.
- In parenting content, include fathers, adoptive parents, non-gestational parents, and other caregivers. Do not assume every reader gave birth or that everyone who gives birth is a woman. Distinguish factors specific to pregnancy and postpartum recovery from experiences shared across early caregiving.

Use this as a flexible rhythm, not a mandatory template:

1. Open inside a specific, recognizable moment: the clock says 3 AM, the reader is staring at the ceiling, or thoughts arrive as soon as their head touches the pillow.
2. Ask a direct question that makes the reader feel seen.
3. Normalize the experience and make a clear promise about what the article will explain.
4. Explain the science in short sections with lively, memorable headings.
5. Translate technical concepts into concrete metaphors or ordinary situations.
6. Connect the explanation to the reader's behavior without blaming them.
7. Give a small number of practical, realistic actions.
8. End by restoring hope and agency.
9. When appropriate, invite the reader to seek professional support with a gentle, specific call to action.

## Rhythm and mechanics

- Mix short, emphatic sentences with medium-length explanatory paragraphs.
- Prefer one idea per paragraph and use white space generously.
- Use rhetorical questions to anticipate what the reader is thinking.
- Speak in first person when useful: “déjame explicarte,” “I know,” or “my answer is.”
- Use occasional asides in parentheses for intimacy or humor.
- Use numbered or bulleted lists when they make advice easier to apply.
- Write memorable headings with personality, not generic labels. Examples of the pattern include “El cortisol olvidó el reloj” and “The Prefrontal Cortex Clocked Out Hours Ago.”
- Repeat the central idea strategically, but remove filler and redundant summaries.

## Explaining clinical information

- Start with the plain-language explanation; introduce the technical term only when it helps.
- Define jargon immediately and use the Spanish and English clinical terms appropriate to each audience, such as TCC-I and CBT-I.
- Use analogies that clarify rather than distort. Examples include an amygdala behaving like an overly sensitive fire alarm or sleep pressure behaving like appetite.
- Distinguish normal human experiences from symptoms that are persistent enough to merit professional evaluation.
- Attribute statistics and clinical recommendations to credible sources. Include references when the claim is specific, consequential, or not common knowledge.
- Do not diagnose the reader. Avoid categorical statements such as “you have insomnia” unless the surrounding language clearly explains that only a qualified professional can diagnose it.
- Do not imply that general educational advice replaces individualized medical or mental-health care.

## Spanish voice

Spanish copy must sound originally written in Spanish, not translated from English.

- Use a broadly understandable Latin American Spanish with natural Colombian/Latino conversational touches when they fit.
- Address the reader with **tú**, not *usted*.
- Follow the site's established inclusive-language convention using **x** where gender-neutral wording is not natural: *cansadx*, *solx*, *humanx*. Prefer an already neutral construction when it reads smoothly.
- Use idiomatic phrases sparingly and naturally: *hacer el oso*, *la vieja confiable*, *para rematar*, *darle vueltas*, *¿y entonces qué hago?*
- Allow warmth and playful emphasis: *tu camita*, *mis amigos*, *adivina*, *OTRA VEZ*.
- Keep clinical names accurate: *Terapia Cognitivo-Conductual para el Insomnio (TCC-I)*, *ansiedad generalizada*, *ritmo biológico*.
- Avoid stiff translations, Spain-specific phrasing, unexplained Anglicisms, and exaggerated inspirational language.
- Localize examples and jokes instead of translating them word for word.

## English voice

English copy should feel natural to a U.S. audience while retaining Mónica's warmth and personality.

- Use contractions and direct address.
- Favor clear conversational phrases such as “the second your head touches the pillow,” “clocked out,” “broken record,” and “there's a way out.”
- Use light, familiar humor, but do not become flippant or overly clever.
- Explain the science with the same care as in Spanish; do not make English the more formal version.
- Avoid corporate wellness language, therapy clichés, and unnecessarily academic vocabulary.

## Bilingual adaptation

- Do not translate sentence by sentence.
- Preserve the purpose, emotional arc, clinical meaning, and level of intimacy.
- Rewrite metaphors, humor, headings, and calls to action so they feel native in each language.
- Keep facts, qualifications, citations, and clinical boundaries equivalent across versions.
- SEO titles and descriptions may differ when the natural search phrase differs by language.

## Humor and personality

Humor should create recognition and lower the reader's defensiveness. It should never make the symptom, diagnosis, culture, or reader the joke.

Good patterns:

- Personifying a process: cortisol forgetting the clock.
- Comparing a symptom to an unwanted guest or a stubborn relative.
- Naming an absurd but familiar habit, such as repeatedly checking the clock.
- A brief cultural reference that genuinely clarifies the concept.

Avoid:

- Forced jokes in every section.
- Sarcasm directed at the reader.
- Humor around trauma disclosures, identity, or severe symptoms.
- Internet slang that will quickly date the article unless the context truly calls for it.

## Calls to action

- Transition naturally from education to support; do not abruptly switch into sales language.
- Reinforce that the reader does not have to solve the problem alone.
- Name the relevant service and mention bilingual care when useful.
- Prefer a low-pressure next step, such as a free 15-minute consultation.
- Never manufacture urgency, fear, scarcity, or guaranteed results.

## Words and patterns to favor

- “No hay ningún problema contigo.” / “There's nothing wrong with you.”
- “¿Ahora tiene más sentido?” / “Does it make more sense now?”
- “No tienes que resolverlo solx.” / “You don't have to figure it out alone.”
- Concrete sensory details, recognizable nighttime thoughts, and ordinary examples.
- Clear transitions such as “Pero ahí no acaba todo,” “Aquí está la parte…,” and “Okay, so what do I do?”

Use these as tonal references, not phrases to insert mechanically. Avoid repeating signature wording so often that it becomes formulaic.

## Avoid

- Sounding like a generic clinic, medical encyclopedia, motivational speaker, or sales funnel.
- Long openings that delay the reader's real question.
- Dense jargon, passive constructions, and unnecessary qualifications.
- Shame-based language or suggesting that the reader caused their condition.
- Advice presented as simple when financial, cultural, family, work, disability, or immigration realities may complicate it.
- Claims that exceed the cited evidence.
- Translating idioms literally.
- Adding personal history, client stories, credentials, or clinical opinions that Mónica has not supplied or approved.

## Drafting checklist

Before delivering a draft, confirm:

- The opening depicts a real moment the intended reader recognizes.
- The reader feels understood before being instructed.
- Every technical term is explained plainly.
- Humor supports the message and sounds like Mónica.
- Recommendations are specific, realistic, and clinically responsible.
- The conclusion restores agency without promising an outcome.
- The call to action is relevant and low-pressure.
- Spanish and English versions each sound native rather than mirrored.
- Factual claims and references have been verified when needed.
- No personal or clinical detail has been invented.

## Maintaining this guide

Mónica's direct feedback overrides any generalization in this file. When she identifies language she would always use, never use, or revise, update this guide with the durable principle rather than copying a one-off preference without context.
