Most people understand that stress makes it hard to sleep. What's less understood is the other direction: poor sleep makes you more reactive to stress — and that feedback loop is one of the most common reasons insomnia becomes chronic.

## How stress affects sleep

When your nervous system perceives threat — a deadline, a difficult conversation, financial worry — it activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, triggering the release of cortisol and adrenaline. This is your fight-or-flight response. It's useful when you actually need to run from something.

At bedtime, it's a problem.

Cortisol increases alertness and suppresses melatonin. Your mind races. Your body stays physiologically aroused. Sleep becomes something you're *trying* to do rather than something that happens naturally — and effort is the enemy of sleep.

## How poor sleep amplifies stress

Here's where the loop closes. Sleep deprivation raises your cortisol baseline, making your stress response faster to trigger and slower to recover. Your threat-detection system becomes hypersensitive. Problems that would normally feel manageable feel insurmountable.

You also lose access to the resources that help you cope: patience, perspective, emotional regulation, creativity. Sleep-deprived decision-making looks remarkably like stressed decision-making — because neurologically, they overlap significantly.

## Why "sleep more" isn't sufficient advice

If stress is keeping you awake and poor sleep is sustaining your stress response, telling someone to "just get more sleep" solves nothing. You need to interrupt the loop, not just address one end of it.

This is where therapy becomes more effective than sleep hygiene tips alone.

## What actually helps

**Addressing the cognitive layer.** Racing thoughts at bedtime are rarely random — they follow predictable patterns. Learning to identify and interrupt rumination (particularly the 3am spiral) changes the relationship between your mind and sleep.

**Nervous system regulation.** Practices like diaphragmatic breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, and reducing physiological arousal before bed can lower the cortisol load that's keeping you alert.

**Sleep as a non-negotiable.** People under stress often de-prioritize sleep in favor of productivity. This is counterproductive: the research consistently shows that cognitive performance, emotional regulation, and decision-making all deteriorate with poor sleep in ways that more hours of wakefulness can't compensate for.

**Treating insomnia directly.** When the stress cycle has been running long enough, the insomnia takes on a life of its own — independent of the original stressor. This is when CBT-I becomes relevant: it targets the insomnia itself, not just the stress.

---

Breaking the loop takes more than one good night. But it's possible — and most people who commit to treatment see meaningful improvement within a few weeks.
