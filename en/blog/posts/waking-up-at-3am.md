You wake up, check the clock, and what do you find? It's 3 am. Wide awake, replaying that embarrassing thing you did three decades ago on a loop. But why?

If this happens to you often, you're not imagining it, and it has a solution. But first, let me explain what happens at 3 am and why it's so hard to fall back asleep.

## Why do I wake up at 3 am?

You've probably heard about cortisol and all the things it does for your body. But it's gotten a pretty bad reputation. Cortisol is one of the hormones that helps your body wake up and start the day, and it runs on its own rhythm. It doesn't need an alarm clock: cortisol, like many hormones, follows your biological clock, and that clock starts ramping up well before you wake up, around 3-4 am. That said, this alone isn't enough of a reason to wake you up and keep you from falling back asleep. Otherwise, all of us would be up at 3 am every night.

## Light sleep plays a role too: what your brain is doing at 3 am

Many people think sleep is a state where our brain shuts off completely. What I just described, my friends, is called a coma. There's a lot more going on than we realize. Our sleep isn't the same all night long. You've probably heard about the different sleep stages (REM, non-REM…) and the obsession with getting more deep sleep. But contrary to what a lot of people think, and contrary to what a lot of products try to sell you, deep sleep isn't something you can fully control, though you can influence it to some degree.

Most of that deep sleep, the most restorative kind, happens in the first half of the night. It's the stage where the world could fall down around you and you wouldn't notice. As the hours go on, your sleep shifts into lighter stages, like REM, which is when we dream. This stage is also when emotional processing happens, which is hugely important for mental health.

A study out of Harvard and UC Berkeley (Yoo et al., 2007) found that a single night of total sleep loss produced a 60% increase in amygdala reactivity to negative stimuli. For anyone who's seen *Inside Out*, think of the amygdala as the emotional command center. Other studies, focused specifically on REM sleep deprivation, show the same pattern: when REM is reduced or fragmented, reactive emotions — anxiety, anger — take over. This is why 3 am thoughts, and not being able to fall back asleep, feel like a threat. You're at a cortisol peak — completely normal — and, on top of that, right in the middle of emotional processing, a time when everything can feel amplified.

**In short, you're not sleeping for three reasons:**

- Elevated cortisol around 3 am.
- Your brain is processing emotional stuff.
- You've moved past deep sleep and into lighter sleep stages.

## The problem isn't waking up at 3 am

There's nothing wrong with your brain or your body. You haven't forgotten how to sleep, and waking up at 3 am doesn't mean you won't be able to sleep well again. The problem is what you do once you're awake at 3 am — that's what keeps you up. Want to learn more? Check out [our previous post about what contributes to develop insomnia](https://greywellness.com/en/blog/posts/why-does-insomnia-happen/).

## So what do I do when I wake up at 3 am?

Would you force yourself to eat more when you're already full? So why would you force yourself to sleep when you've already had enough, or simply can't sleep anymore? Here's what you can do when you've run out of appetite for sleep:

- **Wait 20 minutes; if you can't fall back asleep, get out of bed.** A lot of people ask me how to know if 20 minutes have passed. My answer is always: if you're feeling frustrated or annoyed, that's your sign.
- **Don't watch the clock.** Who has ever fallen asleep faster by watching the clock? I haven't met that person. It usually just creates more anxiety and worry. Counting how many hours you have left before the alarm goes off doesn't help anyone.
- **Relax and enjoy that time with yourself.** Try deep breathing, relaxing music, reading… Think of sleep like being asked to give a urine sample for a medical test: the more you think about it, the less you're able to go. It's all pressure!
- **Reframe your thoughts.** Waking up once in a blue moon doesn't mean you have insomnia. Don't stress about it! Those very worry-thoughts are part of what turns one bad night of sleep into insomnia. Let yourself believe that one night doesn't mean there's a problem. And if the sleepless nights keep piling up, see a sleep professional — like me!

Waking up at 3 am every once in a while doesn't mean you have a problem. It means you're human! That doesn't make it any less stressful or tedious. Give yourself permission to normalize it, and to get help if it gets worse. Next time you wake up at 3 am, remember this article: there's nothing wrong with you. You're human!
